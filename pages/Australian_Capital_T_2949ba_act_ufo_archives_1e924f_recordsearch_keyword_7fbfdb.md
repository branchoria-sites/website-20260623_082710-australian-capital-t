## Introduction

One of the easiest ways to miss Australian UFO records is to search the National [Archives]({{ 'archives/' | relative_url }}) of Australia’s RecordSearch database for the word “UFO” alone. Many Commonwealth files were created by Defence, the Department of Air, the Royal Australian Air Force (RAAF) and other agencies that preferred bureaucratic labels such as “Unusual Aerial Sightings” rather than the popular language of flying saucers or UFOs. As a result, researchers following the ACT-centred archival trail can mistakenly conclude that few records exist when, in reality, they are hidden behind official filing terminology. The National Archives itself notes that the RAAF collected and investigated reports under administrative systems that often used “Unusual Aerial Sightings” classifications rather than modern UFO language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naa.gov.au/blog/flying-saucers-fact-or-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naa.gov.au">[NAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">flying saucers fact or fiction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Flying saucers – fact or fiction?28 Feb 2018 — The disappearance of an aircraft while the pilot was reporting a UFO created a media se...</span></span></span>

## Why Official Wording Matters

Government agencies rarely file records according to the language used by newspaper headlines. During the Cold War, officials were concerned with air defence, aviation safety and intelligence collection, not with promoting the idea of extraterrestrial spacecraft. Reports therefore entered administrative systems under terms that reflected those concerns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naa.gov.au/blog/flying-saucers-fact-or-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naa.gov.au">[NAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">flying saucers fact or fiction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Flying saucers – fact or fiction?28 Feb 2018 — The disappearance of an aircraft while the pilot was reporting a UFO created a media se...</span></span></span>

The most important example is “Unusual Aerial Sightings” (often abbreviated to UAS). Historical Australian government records show that this phrase became a standard administrative label for reports that the public would usually call UFO sightings. Researchers who search only for “UFO” can therefore miss entire groups of files catalogued under UAS terminology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_ufology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian ufology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Australian ufology</span></span></span>

This matters particularly in the ACT context because RecordSearch is the principal gateway to Commonwealth archival holdings. The database reflects the language used when records were created, not necessarily the language modern researchers expect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naa.gov.au/help-your-research/getting-started/recordsearch-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naa.gov.au">[NAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RecordSearch: an overviewRecordSearch is the National Archives&#x27; collection database. ## How Absence Can Mislead Researchers

A failed search is not always evidence that records do not exist.

RecordSearch reflects archival descriptions created over many decades. Some file titles are brief, some use abbreviations, and some were catalogued before modern search practices became common. Consequently, a narrow keyword search can produce a misleadingly empty result set.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.naa.gov.au/help-your-research/getting-started/recordsearch-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naa.gov.au">[NAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RecordSearch: an overviewRecordSearch is the National Archives&#x27; collection database. This problem has occasionally fuelled claims that records were destroyed, hidden or never existed. In some cases, the simpler explanation is that the records were filed under unexpected terminology. Researchers who broaden their searches from “UFO” to “Unusual Aerial Sightings”, agency names, policy references and related Defence subjects often uncover material that was invisible in a basic search.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/kbcat/kb_uasgov.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[project1947.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Unusual Aerial Sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Search Through The...Keith Basterfield documents the results of his research into Australian Government files relating to “Unusual Aer...</span></span></span>

The issue is especially important for readers exploring the ACT’s role in UFO history.
